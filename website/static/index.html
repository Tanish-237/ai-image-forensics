Digital Media Fingerprinting and Source Identification

Is this image AI-generated?

Every camera leaves a fingerprint in the pixels, and so does every image generator. We built and stress-tested detectors that look for these traces, from 2021 generators to ChatGPT's GPT Image 2.

Try it

Upload a photo or an AI image (JPEG, PNG, WebP or iPhone HEIC, up to 25 MB); nothing is stored. The file is checked in two ways: its provenance, the signed Content Credentials and labels that services such as ChatGPT embed, and its pixels, scored in a grid of 224 × 224 tiles at original resolution.

The verdict, the provenance of the file, the pixel detector's score and a map of which regions look generated will appear here.

Analysing… this runs on a CPU and can take up to 30 seconds.

    Which AI? ?

      Research prototype. Missing Content Credentials do not mean that an image is real: messaging apps, screenshots and most editors remove them. Our experiments show that pixel detectors are less reliable on unfamiliar generators and on images that were resized, compressed or shared. Do not use a single result as proof.

      What we found

      Our experiments on Tiny GenImage (six generators from 2021–23), OpenFake (generators from 2024–26), real photographs from 57 phone models and images from ChatGPT and Gemini. All numbers below are our own measurements.

      Shortcuts

      A detector can score 98.8% without really working

      Real photos in public datasets are JPEGs while AI images are often PNGs. A model trained on such data learned that JPEG traces mean “real”: once the AI images were compressed the same way, its accuracy fell from 98.8% to 78.9%. We removed these shortcuts before every other experiment.

      Fingerprints

      Each generator leaves a visible pattern

      Averaging the frequency spectrum of the noise left after removing image content shows a distinct pattern for every generator. Our attribution model names the right one of seven sources 86.1% of the time (chance: 14.3%).

      New generators

      Unseen generators are the real challenge

      Trained on one generator, every detector caught about 97% of its images but only 55–80% of five others. The CLIP-based detector generalised best.

      Processing

      Resizing makes real photos look fake

      After shrinking images to half size, the detectors still flagged AI images, but called only 1–6% of real photographs real. Removing fine detail and camera noise makes a photo resemble a generated image.

      Then vs now

      2023 detectors miss many 2026 images

      On images from 2024–26 generators, ROC-AUC fell from 0.99 to 0.72–0.75. GPT Image 2, released in April 2026, was detected only 14–34% of the time.

      WhatsApp

      One share is enough to confuse them

      We sent 100 images through WhatsApp ourselves. It re-saved every image as a JPEG of quality about 80 and shrank large ones; afterwards no detector did much better than chance.

      Keeping up

      Recent training data closes most of the gap

      Retrained on generators from 2022–25, the CLIP-based detector reached a ROC-AUC of 0.95 on the unseen 2024–26 generators and 0.85 after WhatsApp. A detector is only as current as its training data.

      Whole image

      More data helps; looking at the whole image helps more after sharing

      With 17,780 training images from 74 generators, the CLIP detector reached 0.96 on 2024–26 generators. Scoring a grid of tiles over the whole image instead of one centre crop raised its ROC-AUC after WhatsApp from 0.85 to 0.93. Training with random blur, resizing and compression did not help in our setting.

      Benchmark vs real world

      A 0.999 benchmark score, and most real photos called AI

      A detector trained on every recent generator in OpenFake reached a ROC-AUC of 0.999 on its test set, yet it called only 45% of phone photos and 28% of camera photos real: all of its real training images came from two photo collections. Adding photos from 57 phone models fixed this. Our final detector calls 98% of Android and iPhone photos real and detects 97% of ChatGPT and 99% of Gemini images.

      Provenance

      ChatGPT signs its images, until they are shared

      Images downloaded from ChatGPT carry Content Credentials (C2PA): a record that says “created by ChatGPT, a trained model”, signed by OpenAI and tied to the exact pixels. We verified them against the official C2PA trust list; changing a single pixel breaks the signature. WhatsApp removed the record from the ChatGPT image we sent, so the pixel detector is still needed.

      Our own test

      53 of 62 of our own AI images caught, including screenshots and WhatsApp copies

      After training was finished we generated 16 new images with ChatGPT, Gemini and Grok, on phones and on a desktop, and tested each one as downloaded, as a screenshot, after WhatsApp and as a screenshot sent through WhatsApp. Every original was identified, mostly from its Content Credentials (ChatGPT and Gemini sign their images; Grok signed only the desktop downloads, with an unverifiable self-signed certificate). Without that record the pixel detector still caught 50 of 62 files: all 16 from Grok and 14 of 16 from ChatGPT. Its weak spot was photorealistic outdoor scenes from Gemini, such as a street, a lake at sunset and a lion.

      How it works

      1. Clean the data. Same file format, same JPEG quality range and the same 224 × 224 crop position for real and AI images, so that only pixel content can separate them.
      2. Three generations of detectors. A fine-tuned ResNet-50 (Wang et al., 2020), frozen CLIP features with a linear classifier (Ojha et al., 2023) and NPR, which looks at differences between neighbouring pixels (Tan et al., 2024), plus their combination.
      3. Stress tests. Unseen generators, JPEG, resizing and blur, 2024–26 generators from OpenFake, and real sharing through WhatsApp.
      4. Attribution. A seven-class model that names the source of an image.
      5. This website. First the file's provenance is read. ChatGPT and a growing number of other generators and cameras attach Content Credentials (C2PA): a record of how the image was made, signed with a certificate. If the signature is valid and the image has not been changed since signing, the record decides the verdict. Unsigned AI labels in the metadata are also reported. If there is no such evidence, which is the case for any image that was shared through a messaging app, the pixels decide: the image is split into a grid of up to nine tiles at full resolution, each tile is scored by a CLIP-based detector, and the scores are averaged. The detector was trained on about 89,000 images: OpenFake (including GPT Image 1.5 and 2, Nano Banana Pro and Grok’s Aurora), real photographs from 57 Android and iPhone models and other cameras, and ChatGPT and Gemini images, each also in a simulated WhatsApp-shared version. Its threshold was calibrated on separate images.
