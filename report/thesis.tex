\prefacesection{Abstract}
AI image generators now produce photorealistic images, which raises a
forensic question: was an image captured by a camera, or produced by a
generative model, and if so, by which one? This project treats
AI-generated image detection as a \emph{source identification} problem.
The generator plays the role that a camera plays in classical media
forensics, and it is expected to leave its own statistical fingerprint in
the pixels. We evaluate detectors in four stages: real-versus-AI
detection, generalization to generators unseen during training,
robustness to common processing (JPEG compression, resizing, cropping)
and attribution to a specific generator.

A pilot study on our own images shows why careful dataset design is
essential. Real and AI images could be separated with 100\% accuracy
using file properties alone, without any pixel analysis. After
normalization, every such shortcut fell to chance level (50\%).

\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Motivation}
Online platforms, newsrooms and courts increasingly need to know where an
image came from. Generative models such as diffusion models now produce
images that are hard to tell apart from photographs by eye.

Metadata cannot answer this question reliably. In our pilot data, every
real iPhone photo carried 11 EXIF tags naming the camera, while every AI
image carried none. A screenshot or a messaging app strips such
metadata from a real photo, and an AI image can be re-saved with any
metadata at all. Active approaches, such as watermarks or signed
provenance records, work only when the creator cooperates and the record
survives sharing. This project therefore follows the \emph{passive
forensics} approach: it looks for traces that the image-creation process
leaves in the pixel data unintentionally~\cite{verdoliva2020media}.

\section{Problem Statement}
Given only the pixels of an image, decide whether it was produced by a
camera or by a generative model and, where possible, identify the
generator. The decision should remain reliable for generators not seen
during training and after common image processing.

\section{Contributions}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A de-biasing procedure for Tiny GenImage that matches real and
        generated images in format, resolution, colour mode and JPEG
        compression, including quality-factor matching and
        grid-aligned cropping.
  \item A measurement of shortcut learning: the same detector trained on
        the original (biased) and on the de-biased data, evaluated on the
        same test set.
  \item A comparison of three generations of detectors (a fine-tuned
        CNN, frozen CLIP features and the NPR forensic residual) and a
        simple combination of the last two, on known and unseen
        generators and under common image processing.
  \item A ``then versus now'' study: detectors trained on 2021--2023
        generators tested on 2025--2026 generators, and, conversely,
        detectors trained on current generators.
  \item A real-world sharing test in which real and generated images are
        sent through WhatsApp before detection.
  \item Visualisations of generator fingerprints in the frequency domain
        and in the NPR residual.
  \item A demo application in which users upload an image and see the
        detector's prediction.
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Background and Related Work}

\section{Digital Image Forensics}
Every step that creates or processes an image leaves statistical
traces. A \emph{trace} is any such footprint; a \emph{fingerprint} is a
trace that is consistent across all images from one source, distinctive
between sources and reasonably stable under normal processing. In a
camera, the lens, colour filter array, sensor, demosaicing, in-camera
processing and JPEG compression each leave characteristic traces.

The best-known fingerprint is the photo-response non-uniformity (PRNU)
of the camera sensor. Luk\'{a}\v{s} \emph{et al.}~\cite{lukas2006prnu}
extract a noise residual (an image minus its denoised version), average
residuals over many images from one camera to obtain a reference pattern
and identify the camera of a new image by correlation.

\section{Generator Fingerprints}
A fully AI-generated image never passes through a lens or sensor.
Instead, the generator's architecture and training can leave traces of
their own. Marra \emph{et al.}~\cite{marra2019gans} showed that GANs
leave model-specific fingerprints, in direct analogy with camera PRNU.
This analogy is the basis of the project: detecting and attributing AI
images is source identification in which the source may be a
generative model.

\section{Learned Detectors}
Wang \emph{et al.}~\cite{wang2020cnn} trained a standard image
classifier (ResNet-50) on images from a single GAN and reported that,
with suitable pre-processing and data augmentation, it generalized to
other CNN-based generators. Ojha \emph{et al.}~\cite{ojha2023universal}
observed that detectors trained in this way fail to generalize to newer
families of generative models. They proposed classifying images in the
feature space of a pre-trained CLIP model that was never trained for
real-versus-fake detection.

Tan \emph{et al.}~\cite{tan2024npr} argued that the up-sampling layers
used by both GANs and diffusion models leave local correlations between
neighbouring pixels. Their Neighbouring Pixel Relationships (NPR)
representation subtracts from an image a copy that has been
down-sampled and up-sampled again, which removes most scene content and
keeps these local traces. A shallow residual network (the first two
stages of a ResNet-50) is then trained from scratch on the NPR
representation instead of the raw image.

\section{Datasets and Dataset Bias}
GenImage~\cite{zhu2023genimage} is a benchmark of about 1.33 million
real images (from ImageNet) and 1.35 million images from eight
generators, with tasks for cross-generator and degraded-image
classification. Grommelt \emph{et al.}~\cite{grommelt2024fake} showed
that GenImage contains JPEG-compression and image-size biases, and that
detectors trained on it learn these biases. They reported that removing
the biases improved robustness to JPEG compression and raised
cross-generator performance by more than 11 percentage points for
ResNet-50 and Swin-T detectors.

OpenFake~\cite{livernoche2025openfake} is a recent benchmark that pairs
real photographs with images from current proprietary and open-source
generators. Its test split contains only generators that are absent
from its training split, including GPT Image 1.5, Midjourney 7 and
Flux.2, together with real images from ImageNet and DOCCI.

The two classes are balanced (14{,}000 real and 14{,}000 generated

pilot study. Every real image is a JPEG and every generated image is a
PNG, so the rule ``PNG means fake'' labels all 35{,}000 images correctly.
Image size is also tied to the source: each generator produces a fixed
resolution, so size alone separates BigGAN, Midjourney and the real
images from the rest.

\section{Bias Control}
The inventory showed that file format, resolution and colour mode each
reveal the source of an image. We therefore rebuilt the dataset so that
real and generated images differ only in their pixel content.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Filtering.} BigGAN was removed because its
        $128\times128$ images cannot be cropped to the $224\times224$
        input size without resizing. We also removed the 280 grayscale
        real images and real images whose shorter side is below 224
        pixels. ADM's alpha channel was dropped by converting every image
        to three-channel RGB. In total, 31{,}108 of 35{,}000 images
        remained.
  \item \textbf{Cropping.} Every image was center-cropped to
        $224\times224$ pixels. We crop rather than resize, because
        resizing alters the fine pixel-level traces that detectors rely
        on. The crop offset is rounded down to a multiple of 16 pixels,
        so that the $8\times8$ JPEG block grid (and the $16\times16$
        chroma blocks) of the real images stays aligned with the grid of
        the generated images.
  \item \textbf{Compression matching.} All real images are JPEG files and
        all generated images are lossless PNG files. Following the
        approach of Grommelt \emph{et al.}~\cite{grommelt2024fake}, we
        estimated the JPEG quality factor of each real image by matching
        its luminance quantization table against the standard tables
        scaled for qualities 1 to 100. Each generated image was then
        compressed once with a quality factor sampled from the
        distribution of real training images. After this step, every image
        in both classes has been JPEG-compressed exactly once.
  \item \textbf{Splitting and balancing.} The dataset's training split
        was divided into our training (85\%) and validation (15\%) sets,
        stratified by source. The dataset's own validation split was kept
        as an untouched test set. Within each set, real images were
        sub-sampled to match the number of generated images.
\end{enumerate}

All real images used standard JPEG quantization tables. Their quality
factor is concentrated at 96 (12{,}298 of 17{,}500 images, 70\%), with
smaller groups at 75, 80, 90, 92 and 85. Table~\ref{tab:splits} gives the
final split sizes and confirms that the median quality factor is the same
(96) for both classes in every split.

\section{Detection Methods}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{M1, trained CNN:} a ResNet-50 pre-trained on ImageNet
        and fine-tuned for real-versus-fake classification, following
        the approach of Wang \emph{et al.}~\cite{wang2020cnn}.
  \item \textbf{M2, frozen CLIP features:} image features from a
        pre-trained CLIP model with a linear classifier on top, following
        Ojha \emph{et al.}~\cite{ojha2023universal}.
  \item \textbf{M3, NPR:} the neighbouring pixel relationship
        representation of Tan \emph{et al.}~\cite{tan2024npr}, fed to a
        shallow ResNet (the first two stages of ResNet-50) trained from
        scratch, as in the authors' released code.
  \item \textbf{M4, combined detector:} the output scores of M2 and M3
        combined, so that a semantic detector and a pixel-trace detector
        contribute to one decision. % TODO: final combination rule.
\end{itemize}

The three published methods represent three stages of the field: a
detector trained on raw pixels (2020), a detector built on a large
pre-trained vision model (2023), and a detector built on a forensic
residual (2024).

\section{Training Procedure}
All trained detectors share one training procedure. Images are scaled
to $[0,1]$, normalised with the ImageNet channel means and standard
deviations, and flipped horizontally at random during training. The
network outputs a single score per image, which is passed through a
sigmoid to give the probability that the image is generated. Training
minimises the binary cross-entropy loss with the AdamW optimiser
(learning rate $10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$), batches of 64 images,
mixed-precision arithmetic and a fixed random seed (42), for 5 epochs.
After every epoch the model is evaluated on the validation set, and the
checkpoint with the lowest validation loss is kept. The test set is used
once, with that checkpoint.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
We report four measures on the test set:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Accuracy:} the fraction of images classified correctly
        when an image is called generated if its probability is at least
        0.5.
  \item \textbf{ROC-AUC:} the probability that a randomly chosen
        generated image receives a higher score than a randomly chosen
        real image. It does not depend on the 0.5 threshold, so it
        separates how well a detector ranks images from where its
        threshold lies.
  \item \textbf{Average precision (AP):} the area under the
        precision--recall curve, commonly reported in the detection
        literature.
  \item \textbf{Per-source accuracy:} accuracy computed separately for
        real images and for each generator, so that a weak generator is
        not hidden by the overall average.
\end{itemize}
Misclassified images are inspected in the error analysis.

\subsection*{M2: frozen CLIP features with logistic regression}
The CLIP ViT-L/14 image encoder (304 million parameters) was kept frozen,
and each image was represented by its 768-dimensional, L2-normalised
image embedding. A logistic-regression classifier was trained on these
embeddings, with its regularisation strength $C$ chosen from
$\{0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 100\}$ by validation loss. The best value was
$C = 100$, although $C = 10$ was almost as good (validation loss 0.082
against 0.081). The trained classifier has only 769 parameters.

M2 reached a test accuracy of 97.5\%, a ROC-AUC of 0.997 and an AP of
0.996, slightly above M1 on every measure (Table~\ref{tab:e1}). It was
also more even across sources: its lowest per-source accuracy was 94.4\%
(ADM), against 91.6\% for M1, and its accuracy on Midjourney images rose
from 93.6\% to 97.8\%. A frozen general-purpose representation with a
linear classifier of 769 parameters therefore matched and slightly
exceeded a fully fine-tuned network with about 23.5 million trainable
parameters on the generators seen during training. ADM was the hardest
generator for both methods.

\subsection*{M3: NPR}
M3 was trained from scratch for 10 epochs with a learning rate of
$2\times10^{-4}$ and batches of 32, the settings used in the authors'
GenImage example. The network has 1.44 million parameters. Unlike M1,
its training and validation losses fell together for most of training
(Figure~\ref{fig:m3-curves}). Validation loss was lowest at epoch 8
(0.130), and that checkpoint was used for testing.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/m3_npr_curves.png}
\caption{M3 training curves. Training and validation loss decrease together until about epoch 8.}
\label{fig:m3-curves}

M3 reached a test accuracy of 96.6\%, a ROC-AUC of 0.994 and an AP of
0.992, close to M1 overall. Its errors, however, are distributed
differently. M3 detected every generator with at least 97.6\%
accuracy, including ADM, the hardest generator for M1 and M2, and it
detected all 500 GLIDE test images. In exchange it misclassified more
real photographs: 5.6\% of real test images were labelled as generated,
compared with 2.8\% for M1 and 2.2\% for M2. A detector that looks only
at local pixel relationships is therefore more sensitive to generated
images but also more likely to raise false alarms on real ones.

\subsection*{Comparison}
On generators seen during training, all three detectors exceed 96\%
accuracy and a ROC-AUC of 0.99, so this setting does not separate them
strongly. M2 is best overall and the most balanced; M3 is the most
sensitive to generated images but has the highest false-alarm rate on
real photographs; M1 lies between them. ADM is the hardest generator for
M1 and M2 but not for M3. The more informative comparison is on
generators the detectors have not seen, which E2 and E6 address.

\section{Visualising Generator Fingerprints}
A single image does not reveal a generator's traces because the scene
dominates. Following the logic of camera fingerprinting, we removed the
scene and averaged over many images, so that content cancels out and
only consistent traces remain.

\subsection*{Frequency spectra of the noise residual}
For each test image we converted it to greyscale, subtracted a copy
blurred with a Gaussian filter ($\sigma = 1$) to obtain the noise
residual, and computed the magnitude of its two-dimensional Fourier
transform. Figure~\ref{fig:spectra} shows the logarithm of the average
spectrum over 500 images per source (top) and its difference from the
real-image average (bottom). The horizontal and vertical lines through
the centre appear in every source, including real images; they come
from the image borders rather than from any generator.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/05_spectra.png}
\caption{Average log spectrum of the noise residual per source (top) and difference from real images (bottom; blue = weaker than real, red = stronger).}
\label{fig:spectra}
\end{figure}

Three observations stand out:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Generated images carry less high-frequency energy than
        real photographs.} The difference maps are mostly blue towards
        the edges, so generated images are smoother at the finest scale.
  \item \textbf{GLIDE has the most distinctive spectrum.} Its energy is
        confined to a diamond-shaped central region, and the corners are
        almost empty. This pattern is consistent with an image that was
        produced at a lower resolution and enlarged. GLIDE was also the
        easiest generator for M1 (99.6\% accuracy).
  \item \textbf{VQDM and, more faintly, Midjourney show a regular grid of
        bright points}, the periodic peaks expected from repeated
        up-sampling. SD15 and Wukong have spectra close to each other and
        close to real images.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{NPR residuals}
Figure~\ref{fig:npr} shows a $64\times64$ region of one test image per
source and the magnitude of its NPR residual. In the real image the
residual follows the edges of the scene. In the SD15 example, a nearly
uniform blue region still produces a dense, regular residual pattern,
which is a trace of the generation process rather than of the content.
Table~\ref{tab:npr} lists the mean absolute NPR value over 500 images per
source. Every generator has a lower value than real images, meaning
neighbouring pixels are more alike in generated images. GLIDE has the
lowest value (3.6 against 9.6 for real images), while SD15 and Wukong are
closest to real images.
