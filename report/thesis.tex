\prefacesection{Abstract}
AI image generators now produce photorealistic images, which raises a
forensic question: was an image captured by a camera, or produced by a
generative model, and if so, by which one? This project treats
AI-generated image detection as a \emph{source identification} problem,
in which the generator plays the role that a camera plays in classical
media forensics. We compare three generations of detectors (a fine-tuned
ResNet-50, frozen CLIP features with a linear classifier and the NPR
forensic residual) on known and unseen generators, under JPEG
compression, resizing and blur, and for attribution to a specific
generator, and then test them on current generators and on images shared
through WhatsApp.

Careful data preparation proved essential. File properties alone
separated our pilot images perfectly, and a detector trained on a
popular benchmark without correction lost 19.9 percentage points
(98.8\% to 78.9\%) once the AI images were compressed like the real
ones. On de-biased data all detectors exceeded 96\% accuracy and
attribution to one of seven sources reached 86.1\%. Detectors trained on
2021--2023 generators fell to a ROC-AUC of 0.72--0.75 on 2024--2026
generators and close to chance after WhatsApp sharing, while resizing
and blur made real photographs look generated. Training on recent
generators and analysing the whole image restored a ROC-AUC of 0.93 after
sharing. A detector with a ROC-AUC of 0.999 on a benchmark still called
most real phone and camera photographs AI-generated; adding real-world
photographs from 57 phone models fixed this, and the final detector
called 98\% of Android and iPhone photographs real while detecting 97\%
of ChatGPT and 99\% of Gemini images. Our website combines this detector
with a check of signed Content Credentials, which identified images
downloaded from ChatGPT and Gemini with certainty but are removed by sharing. On 62 files we generated ourselves with ChatGPT, Gemini and Grok, including screenshots and WhatsApp copies, the website called 92\% AI-generated, but attribution of current generators, which reached 67\% on benchmark images, did not transfer to these files.

\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Motivation}
Online platforms, newsrooms and courts increasingly need to know where an
image came from. Generative models such as diffusion models now produce
images that are hard to tell apart from photographs by eye.

Metadata cannot answer this question reliably. In our pilot data, every
real iPhone photo carried 11 EXIF tags naming the camera, while every AI
image carried none. A screenshot or a messaging app strips such
metadata from a real photo, and an AI image can be re-saved with any
metadata at all. Active approaches, such as watermarks or signed
provenance records, work only when the creator cooperates and the record
survives sharing. This project therefore follows the \emph{passive
forensics} approach: it looks for traces that the image-creation process
leaves in the pixel data unintentionally~\cite{verdoliva2020media}.

\section{Problem Statement}
Given only the pixels of an image, decide whether it was produced by a
camera or by a generative model and, where possible, identify the
generator. The decision should remain reliable for generators not seen
during training and after common image processing.

\section{Contributions}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A de-biasing procedure for Tiny GenImage that matches real and
        generated images in format, resolution, colour mode and JPEG
        compression, including quality-factor matching and
        grid-aligned cropping.
  \item A measurement of shortcut learning: the same detector trained on
        the original (biased) and on the de-biased data, evaluated on the
        same test set.
  \item A comparison of three generations of detectors (a fine-tuned
        CNN, frozen CLIP features and the NPR forensic residual) and a
        simple combination of the last two, on known and unseen
        generators and under common image processing.
  \item A ``then versus now'' study: detectors trained on 2021--2023
        generators tested on 2025--2026 generators, and, conversely,
        detectors trained on current generators.
  \item A real-world sharing test in which real and generated images are
        sent through WhatsApp before detection.
  \item Visualisations of generator fingerprints in the frequency domain
        and in the NPR residual.
  \item A deployment detector trained on current generators and on
        real photographs from 57 phone models, and a measurement of how a
        near-perfect benchmark score can hide a real-world failure.
  \item A website that combines a check of signed Content Credentials
        (C2PA) with full-image pixel analysis, and shows the evidence
        behind each verdict.
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Background and Related Work}

\section{Digital Image Forensics}
Every step that creates or processes an image leaves statistical
traces. A \emph{trace} is any such footprint; a \emph{fingerprint} is a
trace that is consistent across all images from one source, distinctive
between sources and reasonably stable under normal processing. In a
camera, the lens, colour filter array, sensor, demosaicing, in-camera
processing and JPEG compression each leave characteristic traces.

The best-known fingerprint is the photo-response non-uniformity (PRNU)
of the camera sensor. Luk\'{a}\v{s} \emph{et al.}~\cite{lukas2006prnu}
extract a noise residual (an image minus its denoised version), average
residuals over many images from one camera to obtain a reference pattern
and identify the camera of a new image by correlation.

\section{Generator Fingerprints}
A fully AI-generated image never passes through a lens or sensor.
Instead, the generator's architecture and training can leave traces of
their own. Marra \emph{et al.}~\cite{marra2019gans} showed that GANs
leave model-specific fingerprints, in direct analogy with camera PRNU.
This analogy is the basis of the project: detecting and attributing AI
images is source identification in which the source may be a
generative model.

\section{Learned Detectors}
Wang \emph{et al.}~\cite{wang2020cnn} trained a standard image
classifier (ResNet-50) on images from a single GAN and reported that,
with suitable pre-processing and data augmentation, it generalized to
other CNN-based generators. Ojha \emph{et al.}~\cite{ojha2023universal}
observed that detectors trained in this way fail to generalize to newer
families of generative models. They proposed classifying images in the
feature space of a pre-trained CLIP model that was never trained for
real-versus-fake detection.

Tan \emph{et al.}~\cite{tan2024npr} argued that the up-sampling layers
used by both GANs and diffusion models leave local correlations between
neighbouring pixels. Their Neighbouring Pixel Relationships (NPR)
representation subtracts from an image a copy that has been
down-sampled and up-sampled again, which removes most scene content and
keeps these local traces. A shallow residual network (the first two
stages of a ResNet-50) is then trained from scratch on the NPR
representation instead of the raw image.

\subsection*{Provenance records}
A different approach records an image's origin when it is created. The
C2PA specification~\cite{c2pa2025spec} defines \emph{Content
Credentials}: a manifest embedded in the file that lists how the image
was made (for example, created by a trained model) and is signed with a
certificate, together with a hash of the image data. Such a record is
reliable when present and intact, but it depends on the cooperation of
the creating software and is removed by many platforms and editors, so
it complements rather than replaces pixel-based detection.

\section{Datasets and Dataset Bias}
GenImage~\cite{zhu2023genimage} is a benchmark of about 1.33 million
real images (from ImageNet) and 1.35 million images from eight
generators, with tasks for cross-generator and degraded-image
classification. Grommelt \emph{et al.}~\cite{grommelt2024fake} showed
that GenImage contains JPEG-compression and image-size biases, and that
detectors trained on it learn these biases. They reported that removing
the biases improved robustness to JPEG compression and raised
cross-generator performance by more than 11 percentage points for
ResNet-50 and Swin-T detectors.

OpenFake~\cite{livernoche2025openfake} is a recent benchmark that pairs
real photographs with images from current proprietary and open-source
generators. Its test split contains only generators that are absent
from its training split, including GPT Image 1.5, Midjourney 7 and
Flux.2, together with real images from ImageNet and DOCCI.

\section{Overview}
The pipeline has five stages: data collection, normalization, splitting,
model training and evaluation. Every experiment saves its metrics to a
shared results folder, from which the tables in this report and the demo
application are produced.

\section{Dataset}

GenImage with 28{,}000 training and 7{,}000 validation images. Each

The two classes are balanced (14{,}000 real and 14{,}000 generated

pilot study. Every real image is a JPEG and every generated image is a
PNG, so the rule ``PNG means fake'' labels all 35{,}000 images correctly.
Image size is also tied to the source: each generator produces a fixed
resolution, so size alone separates BigGAN, Midjourney and the real
images from the rest.

\section{Bias Control}
\label{sec:bias}
The inventory showed that file format, resolution and colour mode each
reveal the source of an image. We therefore rebuilt the dataset so that
real and generated images differ only in their pixel content.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Filtering.} BigGAN was removed because its
        $128\times128$ images cannot be cropped to the $224\times224$
        input size without resizing. We also removed the 280 grayscale
        real images and real images whose shorter side is below 224
        pixels. ADM's alpha channel was dropped by converting every image
        to three-channel RGB. In total, 31{,}108 of 35{,}000 images
        remained.
  \item \textbf{Cropping.} Every image was center-cropped to
        $224\times224$ pixels. We crop rather than resize, because
        resizing alters the fine pixel-level traces that detectors rely
        on. The crop offset is rounded down to a multiple of 16 pixels,
        so that the $8\times8$ JPEG block grid (and the $16\times16$
        chroma blocks) of the real images stays aligned with the grid of
        the generated images.
  \item \textbf{Compression matching.} All real images are JPEG files and
        all generated images are lossless PNG files. Following the
        approach of Grommelt \emph{et al.}~\cite{grommelt2024fake}, we
        estimated the JPEG quality factor of each real image by matching
        its luminance quantization table against the standard tables
        scaled for qualities 1 to 100. Each generated image was then
        compressed once with a quality factor sampled from the
        distribution of real training images. After this step, every image
        in both classes has been JPEG-compressed exactly once.
  \item \textbf{Splitting and balancing.} The dataset's training split
        was divided into our training (85\%) and validation (15\%) sets,
        stratified by source. The dataset's own validation split was kept
        as an untouched test set. Within each set, real images were
        sub-sampled to match the number of generated images.
\end{enumerate}

All real images used standard JPEG quantization tables. Their quality
factor is concentrated at 96 (12{,}298 of 17{,}500 images, 70\%), with
smaller groups at 75, 80, 90, 92 and 85. Table~\ref{tab:splits} gives the
final split sizes and confirms that the median quality factor is the same
(96) for both classes in every split.

\section{Detection Methods}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{M1, trained CNN:} a ResNet-50 pre-trained on ImageNet
        and fine-tuned for real-versus-fake classification, following
        the approach of Wang \emph{et al.}~\cite{wang2020cnn}.
  \item \textbf{M2, frozen CLIP features:} image features from a
        pre-trained CLIP model with a linear classifier on top, following
        Ojha \emph{et al.}~\cite{ojha2023universal}.
  \item \textbf{M3, NPR:} the neighbouring pixel relationship
        representation of Tan \emph{et al.}~\cite{tan2024npr}, fed to a
        shallow ResNet (the first two stages of ResNet-50) trained from
        scratch, as in the authors' released code.
  \item \textbf{M4, combined detector:} the output scores of M2 and M3
        averaged, so that a semantic detector and a pixel-trace detector
        contribute equally to one decision. M4 needs no additional
        training.
\end{itemize}

The three published methods represent three stages of the field: a
detector trained on raw pixels (2020), a detector built on a large
pre-trained vision model (2023), and a detector built on a forensic
residual (2024).

\section{Training Procedure}
All trained detectors share one training procedure. Images are scaled
to $[0,1]$, normalised with the ImageNet channel means and standard
deviations, and flipped horizontally at random during training. The
network outputs a single score per image, which is passed through a
sigmoid to give the probability that the image is generated. Training
minimises the binary cross-entropy loss with the AdamW optimiser
(learning rate $10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$), batches of 64 images,
mixed-precision arithmetic and a fixed random seed (42), for 5 epochs.
After every epoch the model is evaluated on the validation set, and the
checkpoint with the lowest validation loss is kept. The test set is used
once, with that checkpoint.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
We report four measures on the test set:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Accuracy:} the fraction of images classified correctly
        when an image is called generated if its probability is at least
        0.5.
  \item \textbf{ROC-AUC:} the probability that a randomly chosen
        generated image receives a higher score than a randomly chosen
        real image. It does not depend on the 0.5 threshold, so it
        separates how well a detector ranks images from where its
        threshold lies.
  \item \textbf{Average precision (AP):} the area under the
        precision--recall curve, commonly reported in the detection
        literature.
  \item \textbf{Per-source accuracy:} accuracy computed separately for
        real images and for each generator, so that a weak generator is
        not hidden by the overall average.
\end{itemize}
Misclassified images are inspected in the error analysis.

\section{Web Application}
\label{sec:web}
The project website lets a user upload an image (JPEG, PNG, WebP or
iPhone HEIC) and shows the verdict together with the evidence behind it.
An upload is analysed in two steps:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Provenance.} The file's Content Credentials (C2PA
        manifest) are read and verified against the official C2PA trust
        list (Section~\ref{sec:c2pa}). A valid signed record stating
        that a trained model created the image decides the verdict
        ``AI-generated''; a trusted record of a camera capture decides
        ``real photograph''. Unsigned AI labels in the metadata (the IPTC
        digital source type, Stable Diffusion or ComfyUI generation
        settings) also mark an image as generated. Camera EXIF tags are
        shown but never used, because they are easy to edit.
  \item \textbf{Pixels.} If no usable record exists, the image is covered
        by an evenly spaced grid of tiles of $224\times224$ pixels at its
        original resolution (E8; $4\times4$ by default, $3\times3$ or
        $5\times5$ selectable, Section~\ref{sec:tiles}), each tile
        is scored by the CLIP-based detector of E10, and the scores are
        averaged. The score is rescaled so that the calibrated threshold
        maps to 50\%, and the tile scores are drawn on the image. A score
        between 40\% and 60\% is reported as uncertain, with the side it
        leans to (Section~\ref{sec:unsure}).
\end{enumerate}
Three further functions support demonstrations. A \emph{WhatsApp
simulation} re-saves the uploaded file as in E9 and E10 (long side at
most 1600 pixels, JPEG quality 80, metadata removed), analyses the copy
and shows both results side by side, so that the loss of the Content
Credentials and the change of the pixel score can be seen directly. For
one of our ChatGPT images, the simulated copy
(204\,KB, pixel score 70.8\%) closely matched the copy we had actually
sent through WhatsApp (199\,KB, 70.8\%). Six sample images from our own
test can be analysed with one click, and while a public link is open the
page shows it as a QR code, so that a visitor can analyse a photo from
their own phone.
The backend is written in Python with FastAPI and runs on our own
computer during demonstrations, at about 5--9 seconds per image on a
laptop CPU (of which the detector itself takes 3.1 seconds for 16 tiles); a static copy of the page with all experiment results is
hosted on Hugging Face Spaces and connects to the backend through a
temporary public link. Uploaded images are analysed in memory and not
stored. The trained detectors are published in a Hugging Face model
repository.

Figure~\ref{fig:m1-curves} shows that the training loss fell from 0.25 to

without improving on unseen ones, so most of what it learned in later

\subsection*{M2: frozen CLIP features with logistic regression}
The CLIP ViT-L/14 image encoder (304 million parameters) was kept frozen,
and each image was represented by its 768-dimensional, L2-normalised
image embedding. A logistic-regression classifier was trained on these
embeddings, with its regularisation strength $C$ chosen from
$\{0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 100\}$ by validation loss. The best value was
$C = 100$, although $C = 10$ was almost as good (validation loss 0.082
against 0.081). The trained classifier has only 769 parameters.

M2 reached a test accuracy of 97.5\%, a ROC-AUC of 0.997 and an AP of
0.996, slightly above M1 on every measure (Table~\ref{tab:e1}). It was
also more even across sources: its lowest per-source accuracy was 94.4\%
(ADM), against 91.6\% for M1, and its accuracy on Midjourney images rose
from 93.6\% to 97.8\%. A frozen general-purpose representation with a
linear classifier of 769 parameters therefore matched and slightly
exceeded a fully fine-tuned network with about 23.5 million trainable
parameters on the generators seen during training. ADM was the hardest
generator for both methods.

\subsection*{M3: NPR}
M3 was trained from scratch for 10 epochs with a learning rate of
$2\times10^{-4}$ and batches of 32, the settings used in the authors'
GenImage example. The network has 1.44 million parameters. Unlike M1,
its training and validation losses fell together for most of training
(Figure~\ref{fig:m3-curves}). Validation loss was lowest at epoch 8
(0.130), and that checkpoint was used for testing.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/m3_npr_curves.png}
\caption{M3 training curves. Training and validation loss decrease together until about epoch 8.}
\label{fig:m3-curves}
\end{figure}

M3 reached a test accuracy of 96.6\%, a ROC-AUC of 0.994 and an AP of
0.992, close to M1 overall. Its errors, however, are distributed
differently. M3 detected every generator with at least 97.6\%
accuracy, including ADM, the hardest generator for M1 and M2, and it
detected all 500 GLIDE test images. In exchange it misclassified more
real photographs: 5.6\% of real test images were labelled as generated,
compared with 2.8\% for M1 and 2.2\% for M2. A detector that looks only
at local pixel relationships is therefore more sensitive to generated
images but also more likely to raise false alarms on real ones.

\subsection*{Comparison}
On generators seen during training, all three detectors exceed 96\%
accuracy and a ROC-AUC of 0.99, so this setting does not separate them
strongly. M2 is best overall and the most balanced; M3 is the most
sensitive to generated images but has the highest false-alarm rate on
real photographs; M1 lies between them. ADM is the hardest generator for
M1 and M2 but not for M3. The more informative comparison is on
generators the detectors have not seen, which E2 and E6 address.

M4, the average of the M2 and M3 scores, reached 98.8\% accuracy and a
ROC-AUC of 0.999, the best result in E1, although it required no
training. Real-image accuracy rose to 98.1\% and the detection rate on
generated images to 99.5\%: the two methods make different mistakes,
so averaging their scores corrects many of them.

\section{Generalization to Unseen Generators (E2)}

equal number of real images (3{,}400 training and 600 validation
images), and then tested on the full test set. SD15 is therefore the only
generator seen during training; ADM, GLIDE, Midjourney, VQDM and Wukong
are unseen. Validation also used only SD15 and real images, so that the
unseen generators had no influence on model selection. Because the
training set was six times smaller, M1 was trained for 10 epochs and M3
for 30 epochs, each keeping the checkpoint with the lowest validation
loss. For M2 the regularisation strength was again selected on the
validation set ($C = 100$).

        spectral similarity explains part but not all of the pattern.

M3 also showed unstable validation loss when trained on the smaller
SD15-only set (between 0.15 and 0.54 across epochs), and its best
checkpoint came from epoch 18.

\section{Robustness to Image Processing (E3)}
\label{sec:e3}
Images shared online are rarely the original files. E3 tests the E1
detectors, without retraining, on processed copies of the cleaned test
set. Three kinds of processing were applied, each at several strengths:
JPEG compression at quality 95, 85, 75, 65 and 50; resizing to 90\%,
75\%, 60\% and 50\% of the side length and back to $224\times224$ pixels
(bilinear interpolation); and Gaussian blur with $\sigma$ = 0.5, 1.0,
1.5 and 2.0 pixels. Real and generated images received identical
processing. The unprocessed results reproduce E1 exactly (M1 0.965, M2
0.975, M3 0.966), and M4 is included.

Table~\ref{tab:e3} and Figures~\ref{fig:e3-errors}
and~\ref{fig:e3-auc} show four findings.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Processing makes real photographs look generated.}
        Under resizing and blur the detection rate on generated images
        stayed near 100\%, while the fraction of real photographs
        labelled real collapsed: to between 1\% and 6\% at half size, and
        to between 0\% and 10\% at $\sigma = 1.5$ or more. Accuracy
        therefore fell to about 50\% because the detectors called almost
        everything generated. Resizing and blur remove fine detail and
        sensor noise, and a real photograph without them resembles a
        generated image to these detectors. In practice this is the more
        harmful error, since a genuine photograph would be wrongly
        flagged as fake.
  \item \textbf{M3 (NPR) is the most sensitive to JPEG.} A single
        additional compression at quality 95 reduced M3's real-image
        accuracy from 94.4\% to 13.0\% and its ROC-AUC to 0.788. NPR
        measures differences between neighbouring pixels, which JPEG
        alters in every $8\times8$ block. For M1, stronger compression
        had the opposite effect: real-image accuracy recovered while the
        detection rate on generated images fell to 65.2\% at quality 50,
        so JPEG pushed its scores towards ``real''. The effect of JPEG
        did not increase steadily with lower quality; quality 95 caused
        more false alarms than quality 85 for every method.
  \item \textbf{No single method is the most robust.} M1 was the most
        robust to resizing and strong JPEG in accuracy, M2 had the highest
        accuracy at JPEG quality 85, and M4
        had the highest ROC-AUC under blur and moderate resizing. M2,
        which generalised best to unseen generators in E2, was the most
        affected by mild resizing (real-image accuracy 54.8\% at 90\%
        size).
  \item \textbf{Ranking survives longer than the threshold.} ROC-AUC
        fell much more slowly than accuracy; at 75\% size all methods
        kept a ROC-AUC above 0.93 while their accuracy was 0.71--0.83.
        As in E2, the detectors still ranked most generated images above
        real ones, but processing shifted all scores upwards past the
        0.5 threshold.
\end{enumerate}

Wang \emph{et al.}~\cite{wang2020cnn} reported that adding blur and JPEG
to the training images improves robustness to these operations. Our
detectors were trained without such augmentation, which is a likely
reason for their sensitivity. Cropping was not tested separately:
cropping followed by rescaling to the input size is an enlargement,
which overlaps with the resizing test.

\section{Generator Attribution (E4)}
E4 asks which of the seven sources (real or one of the six generators)
produced an image. Real images were sub-sampled in every split to the
size of one generator class (1{,}700 per class for training, 300 for
validation and 500 for testing), so all seven classes are equally
represented and random guessing scores 14.3\%. Two attribution models
were trained:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A1:} the frozen CLIP features of M2 with a seven-class
        logistic regression. The regularisation strength was selected on
        the validation set ($C = 10$).
  \item \textbf{A2:} ResNet-50 fine-tuned as in M1, with seven outputs,
        a softmax and the cross-entropy loss. Validation loss was lowest
        at epoch 3.
\end{itemize}

  \item \textbf{Generators can be identified from pixels alone.} Both

  \item \textbf{Fine-tuning is better for attribution, unlike for
        generalisation.} A2 was better than A1 for every generator, by
        up to 12.6 points (VQDM). CLIP was best at recognising
        unseen generators in E2, but telling known generators apart
        depends on low-level traces that a fine-tuned network learns and
        that frozen semantic features capture less well. A1 was slightly
        better only on real images (95.8\% against 92.2\%).
  \item \textbf{SD15 and Wukong are confused with each other.} Most
        remaining errors fall between these two generators: 16--19\% of
        SD15 images were attributed to Wukong, and 20--24\% of Wukong
        images to SD15. Their averaged frequency spectra were also the
        most similar (Figure~\ref{fig:spectra}), and Wukong was the
        easiest unseen generator for a detector trained on SD15 in E2.
        Three independent measurements therefore agree that the two
        generators leave similar fingerprints. A1 also confused ADM and
        VQDM (11--12\% in each direction); A2 largely separated them.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Effect of De-biasing (E5)}
E5 measures how much of a detector's accuracy can come from the
compression shortcut described in Section~\ref{sec:bias} rather than
from the image content. We built a second, \emph{uncleaned} copy of the
dataset that skips one step of the bias control: generated images are
cropped but not JPEG-compressed. Everything else is identical, including
the selected images, the crop positions and the splits; the real images
are the same pixel arrays in both copies. On the 3{,}000 generated test
images, the JPEG step changed pixel values by 2.03 levels out of 255 on
average, a difference that is not visible to a human viewer.

        were JPEG-compressed like the real ones, its accuracy fell to
        78.9\%, a drop of 19.9 percentage points. On the fair (cleaned)
        test set it was 17.6 points below the model trained on cleaned
        data.
  \item \textbf{The model had learned ``JPEG traces mean real''.} Its
        accuracy on real images was unchanged (98.5\%), but its
        detection rate on every generator fell, to between 41.8\% (ADM)
        and 71.6\% (GLIDE). Compressing a generated image was enough to
        make it look real to this detector. This matches the finding of
        Grommelt \emph{et al.}~\cite{grommelt2024fake} on the full
        GenImage dataset.

        ROC-AUC on the cleaned test set remained 0.936, well above
        chance, so the model also learned genuine generator traces;
        the shortcut mainly shifted its scores below the threshold.
\end{enumerate}

The model trained on cleaned data also lost accuracy on the uncleaned
test set (96.5\% to 87.0\%), mostly on ADM (51.2\%), Wukong (65.0\%) and
VQDM (68.8\%). Every generated image it saw during training had been
JPEG-compressed, so uncompressed generated images were outside its
training distribution. A mismatch in processing therefore hurts in
both directions, which motivates the robustness tests in E3.

\section{Current Generators and Real-World Sharing (E6)}
\label{sec:e6}
\subsection*{Generators released in 2024--2026}
E6 tests the E1 detectors, without retraining, on generators that are
two to four years newer than those in GenImage. We used the held-out
test split of OpenFake v2~\cite{livernoche2025openfake}, whose generators
do not appear in its own training split and whose real images come from
DOCCI and ImageNet. Three of its thirteen test files were downloaded. We
kept up to 200 images per generator (generators with fewer than 50
usable images were left out) and 300 images from each real source,
applied the same rules as before (no grayscale images, at least 224
pixels per side) and the same 16-pixel-aligned $224\times224$ centre
crop. In total 3{,}091 images were used.

Unlike our cleaned GenImage data, OpenFake was used \emph{as

        DOCCI photographs. DOCCI images are four times larger and more
        strongly compressed, so a $224\times224$ crop shows a magnified,
        smoother region. E3 showed that loss of fine detail makes real
        photographs look generated to these detectors; we consider this
        the likely cause but did not test it separately.

        same generator.} For GPT Image 1.5, M1 detected 16.4\% of PNG
        files and 42.3\% of JPEG files; for Illustrious 43.9\% and
        86.8\%; for Z-Image Turbo 73.1\% and 89.2\%. Our detectors saw
        only JPEG-compressed generated images during training, and E5
        showed that uncompressed generated images are harder for them.
        M2 was the least affected (Illustrious: 92.7\% PNG, 92.1\%
        JPEG).
\end{enumerate}

The evaluation has limitations. Only three of the thirteen OpenFake test
files were used, several generators have fewer than 200 images, and
OpenFake's release dates vary within one generator, so we report the
earliest. Because OpenFake images are larger than GenImage images, the
same $224\times224$ crop covers a smaller part of the scene.

\section{Training on Current Generators (E7)}
E6 showed that detectors trained on 2021--2023 generators miss many
images from 2024--2026 generators. E7 asks whether more recent training
data closes this gap. M1 and M2 were trained again, with unchanged
settings, on data from the OpenFake \emph{training} split: real
photographs from LAION and Pexels and generated images from 72
generators released between 2022 and 2025, including SD~3.5, Flux.1,
Midjourney~6, DALL-E~3 and GPT Image~1. Five training files were chosen
by evenly spaced position in the file list; in this run that selected
one full file and four small ones, giving 3{,}972 balanced training

selection (M1: epoch 2; M2: $C = 10$). The bias control of

compressed once at a quality drawn from the real training images, after
which the median JPEG quality was 95 for real and 91 for generated
training images.

None of the E6 test generators occurs in the OpenFake training split, and
its real test photographs come from different sources (DOCCI and

\section{More Data, Augmentation and Full-Image Analysis (E8)}
\label{sec:e8}
E8 repeats E7 with five complete OpenFake training files (17{,}780
balanced training images from 74 generators; 3{,}446 validation
images) and separates two possible improvements:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Full data:} the larger training set, with each training
        image stored as a $256\times256$ centre crop and a random
        $224\times224$ crop taken at every step.
  \item \textbf{Full data + augmentation:} the same, but each training
        crop is additionally blurred ($\sigma$ from 0.1 to 2), resized to
        50--100\% and back, and JPEG-compressed (quality 50--95), each
        with probability 0.5, following Wang \emph{et al.}~\cite{wang2020cnn}.
        For M2, the classifier is trained on the CLIP features of the
        clean crops and of two augmented copies of each.
\end{itemize}
E8 also tests \textbf{full-image analysis}: instead of one centre crop,
the image is covered by an evenly spaced grid of up to $3\times3$ tiles
of $224\times224$ pixels at its original resolution, and the tile scores
are averaged. Nothing is resized, so pixel traces are preserved.

\section{Provenance Check: Content Credentials}
\label{sec:c2pa}
When we tested an early version of our website on images we had
generated ourselves, the pixel detector missed both ChatGPT images. Their
files, however, carried a different kind of evidence. Both contained a
\emph{Content Credentials} record as defined by the C2PA
specification~\cite{c2pa2025spec}: a manifest stored inside the file
(in a \texttt{caBX} chunk of the PNG) that states how the image was made
and is signed with an X.509 certificate. The manifest of each file
contained:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the action \texttt{c2pa.created} with the software agent
        ``ChatGPT'' (version ``gpt-image'') and the IPTC digital source
        type \texttt{trainedAlgorithmicMedia}, the standard code for media
        created by a trained model;
  \item the signer ``OpenAI OpCo, LLC'' and a signing time;
  \item a hash of the image data, so that any change to the pixels after
        signing can be detected.
\end{itemize}
We verified both files with the open-source C2PA library
(\texttt{c2pa-python}) against the official C2PA trust list of
certificate authorities. Both were reported as \emph{Trusted}: the
signature was valid, the certificate chained to a listed authority and
the image hash matched. When we changed a single pixel and kept the
manifest, the result changed to \emph{Invalid} with the message that the
hashes did not match. The iPhone photograph (HEIC) carried no manifest,
only ordinary EXIF tags (``Apple iPhone 17''), and the copies of all
images received through WhatsApp carried no metadata at all.

Content Credentials are therefore strong evidence when present, but they
are easily lost: WhatsApp, screenshots and most editors remove them, and
a missing record says nothing about an image. The two kinds of evidence
complement each other, and our website uses both
(Section~\ref{sec:web}): a valid signed record decides the verdict; when
there is none, the pixel detector decides. A record that claims a camera
capture is accepted only from a signer on the trust list, because anyone
can sign a false claim with a self-made certificate.

\section{A Detector for Current Generators (E9)}
\label{sec:e9}
E6 to E8 trained only on OpenFake's training split, which leaves out the
newest generators so that generalisation to unseen generators can be
measured. For the website the goal is different: it should work as well
as possible on images that people upload today. E9 therefore trains a
\emph{deployment} detector on everything we can spare. OpenFake test
files 4 to 12, which no earlier experiment used, contribute 62{,}145
images (30{,}386 real from DOCCI and ImageNet, and 31{,}759 generated,
including 3{,}845 GPT Image~1.5, 319 GPT Image~2, 284 Nano Banana Pro and
190 Aurora images), in addition to the E8 training data. Test file 3 is
used to calibrate the threshold and files 0 to 2, the images of E6, for
testing.

Each training image is prepared the way the website sees it: up to four

distributed and once after a simulated WhatsApp share (longest side at
most 1600 pixels, JPEG quality 80, as measured in E6). The detector is
the same as M2: frozen CLIP features and logistic regression with
balanced class weights, fitted on 445{,}112 tiles.

        Imagine'' as the creating software, but signed with a self-signed
        certificate marked ``local use only'', so the signature was valid
        while the signer could not be verified; the Grok images saved on
        the phone carried none. Screenshots and WhatsApp copies never kept
        a manifest.
  \item \textbf{Without credentials the pixel detector caught 50 of 62
        files (81\%).} All 16 Grok files scored above 83\%, including two
        photorealistic images of people, and 14 of 16 ChatGPT files were
        detected.
  \item \textbf{Photorealistic outdoor scenes from Gemini were the
        weakness.} Ten of the twelve pixel-level misses came from three
        Gemini images: a street with caf\'es, a mountain lake at sunset
        and a lion in a savanna, whose originals scored 34--47\%. Gemini's
        indoor scenes scored 67--99\%. Many misses were close to the
        threshold (41--50\% for nine of them).
\end{enumerate}
With the 16-tile grid that the website uses by default since the tile
test of Section~\ref{sec:tiles}, 57 of the 62 files (92\%) were called
AI-generated: 16/16 originals, 13/15 screenshots, 14/16 WhatsApp copies
and 14/15 screenshots sent through WhatsApp (ChatGPT 15/16, Gemini 26/30,
Grok 16/16). All files in this test were generated, so it measures
detection only; real photographs were tested in E10 and in
Section~\ref{sec:tiles}.

\section{Number of Tiles}
\label{sec:tiles}
The website covers an image with a grid of $224\times224$ tiles at full
resolution instead of shrinking the whole image to the detector's input
size, because shrinking removes the fine detail in which generator traces
lie (E3). A $3\times3$ grid, however, covers only about a third of a
typical image. We therefore compared grids of 1 (the centre crop) to 36
tiles on 206 images: the 64 generated files of Section~\ref{sec:fresh}
(our 62 and two earlier ChatGPT images) and 142 real files, namely 70
photographs from 14 phone models that were not used for training (from
Wikimedia Commons), each as downloaded and after a simulated WhatsApp
share, and two of our own iPhone images.

detected many generated files but also called 8.5\% of the real files
AI-generated. The website therefore uses 16 tiles by default and lets the
user choose 9 (faster) or 25.

\section{Uncertain Results}
\label{sec:unsure}
Most of the remaining errors were close calls: of the nine wrong
decisions with 16 tiles in Section~\ref{sec:tiles}, six had a calibrated
score between 40\% and 60\%. The website therefore reports such scores
as ``not sure'' and names the side the score leans to, instead of giving
a confident verdict. The band of ten points on either side of the
decision line was fixed as a round value and not tuned; it was evaluated
on the same 206 files as the number of tiles, so the result is
optimistic.

12 files that would have been decided correctly are also reported as

\section{Attribution of Current Generators (E11)}
\label{sec:e11}

\section{Fingerprint Map (E12)}
\label{sec:e12}
The detectors of E8 to E11 work on CLIP features, 768 numbers per tile.
E12 draws these features as a map. Each test image of E10 and E11 is
represented by the average of its tile features; t-SNE
\cite{vandermaaten2008tsne} places the images in two dimensions so that
images with similar features lie close together. We used up to 300
images per generator family and 600 real photographs (1{,}803 images),
as distributed and after the simulated WhatsApp share. Distances between
distant groups and the size of a group carry little meaning in a t-SNE
map, so the map is backed by a measure computed on the full features:
for each image, the share of its 10 nearest neighbours (cosine distance)
that belong to the same group, which we call \emph{neighbour agreement}.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/e12_fingerprint_map.png}
\caption{E12: CLIP features of 634 real photographs and 1{,}169
generated images, mapped with t-SNE, as distributed and after the
simulated WhatsApp share.}
\label{fig:e12}
\end{figure}

Real photographs and generated images occupy clearly separate regions
(Figure~\ref{fig:e12}): 96\% of the neighbours of a real photograph were
real and 94\% of the neighbours of a generated image were generated,
against 35\% and 65\% by chance. After the simulated share the agreement
fell to 89\% and 85\%, and part of the real photographs moved into the
region of the generated images, in line with E3 and E6: compression and
resizing make a photograph resemble a generated image.

\label{tab:errors}

Across all experiments, four patterns explain most of the errors:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Unfamiliar real photographs are called generated.}
        Resized or blurred photographs (E3), DOCCI photographs for the
        GenImage-trained detectors (E6) and phone photographs for E9 were
        all flagged as AI. Smooth images without sensor noise look
        generated to every detector we trained, and the remedy that
        worked was more varied real training data (E10).
  \item \textbf{Generators with few training images are missed.} The
        weakest generators in E10 had fewer than 300 training images
        each, and in E6 every generator was unseen. Detection of a
        generator depends mainly on whether it, or a close relative, was
        in the training data (E2, E6, E7).
  \item \textbf{Processing hides generator traces.} JPEG compression,
        resizing and sharing through WhatsApp lowered the detection of
        generated images in E3, E6 and E8 and, to a lesser degree, in
        E10.
  \item \textbf{Scores shift before rankings do.} In E2, E3, E6 and E8,
        ROC-AUC stayed much higher than accuracy: the detectors still
        ranked generated images above real ones, but processing or new
        generators moved all scores across the threshold. Calibrating the
        threshold on data like the deployment data (E8, E9, E10) recovered
        most of the accuracy.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Visualising Generator Fingerprints}
A single image does not reveal a generator's traces because the scene
dominates. Following the logic of camera fingerprinting, we removed the
scene and averaged over many images, so that content cancels out and
only consistent traces remain.

\subsection*{Frequency spectra of the noise residual}
For each test image we converted it to greyscale, subtracted a copy
blurred with a Gaussian filter ($\sigma = 1$) to obtain the noise
residual, and computed the magnitude of its two-dimensional Fourier
transform. Figure~\ref{fig:spectra} shows the logarithm of the average
spectrum over 500 images per source (top) and its difference from the
real-image average (bottom). The horizontal and vertical lines through
the centre appear in every source, including real images; they come
from the image borders rather than from any generator.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/05_spectra.png}
\caption{Average log spectrum of the noise residual per source (top) and difference from real images (bottom; blue = weaker than real, red = stronger).}
\label{fig:spectra}
\end{figure}

Three observations stand out:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Generated images carry less high-frequency energy than
        real photographs.} The difference maps are mostly blue towards
        the edges, so generated images are smoother at the finest scale.
  \item \textbf{GLIDE has the most distinctive spectrum.} Its energy is
        confined to a diamond-shaped central region, and the corners are
        almost empty. This pattern is consistent with an image that was
        produced at a lower resolution and enlarged. GLIDE was also the
        easiest generator for M1 (99.6\% accuracy).
  \item \textbf{VQDM and, more faintly, Midjourney show a regular grid of
        bright points}, the periodic peaks expected from repeated
        up-sampling. SD15 and Wukong have spectra close to each other and
        close to real images.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{NPR residuals}
Figure~\ref{fig:npr} shows a $64\times64$ region of one test image per

\chapter{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{chap:conclusion}

\section{Answers to the Research Questions}
\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1: real versus AI on known generators.] Yes. On de-biased data
        all three detectors reached 96.5--97.5\% accuracy and a ROC-AUC
        of at least 0.994, and their combination 98.8\% (E1). This holds
        only when the data is fair: trained on the uncorrected benchmark,
        the same network learned that JPEG traces mean ``real'' and lost
        19.9 percentage points on fairly prepared images (E5).
  \item[RQ2: unseen generators.] Only partly. Trained on one generator,
        the detectors caught 55--80\% of five others (E2), and detectors
        trained on 2021--2023 generators reached a ROC-AUC of only
        0.72--0.75 on 2024--2026 generators; ChatGPT's GPT Image~2 was
        detected 13.5--34.2\% of the time (E6). Frozen CLIP features
        generalised best. Most errors were threshold errors: unseen
        generated images were still ranked above real ones but scored
        below the threshold. Training data from recent generators closed
        most of the gap (ROC-AUC 0.957, E8).
  \item[RQ3: processing.] Processing is the main practical weakness.
        Resizing and blur made real photographs look generated (at half
        size only 1--6\% were called real, E3), and a single WhatsApp
        share brought every detector close to chance (E6). Recent
        training data, full-image analysis and a calibrated threshold
        restored a ROC-AUC of 0.93 after WhatsApp (E8), and the final
        detector reached 0.99 (E10), although with the test generators
        seen in training.
  \item[RQ4: attribution.] Within a benchmark, yes: a fine-tuned
        ResNet-50 named the correct one of seven 2021--2023 sources for
        86.1\% of images (E4), and a CLIP-based model the correct one of
        nine current generator families for 67\% (E11; chance 11\%).
        Each generator also showed a visible, averaged frequency
        fingerprint. For images made with the applications themselves,
        no: the E11 model named the right application for only 5 of our
        62 files. When Content Credentials survive, they name the
        application with certainty. In the CLIP features, real and
        generated images form clearly separate groups, and each family
        is more often its own neighbour than chance (E12).
\end{description}

Two further lessons came from testing on images we made ourselves. A
benchmark score is not a guarantee: a detector with a ROC-AUC of 0.999
on OpenFake called most real phone photographs generated, because all of
its real training images came from two photo collections; real-world
photographs from 57 phone models fixed this (E10). And pixels are not the
only evidence: images downloaded from ChatGPT carry signed Content
Credentials that identify them with certainty, but sharing removes them,
so a pixel detector remains necessary.

\section{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The final detector (E9, E10) was trained on the generators it was
        tested on; its results measure detection of known generators on
        new images, not generalisation, which E2 and E6 measure.
  \item Some current generators had few training images (319 for GPT
        Image~2, 190 for Grok's Aurora), and new generators appear every
        few months; E6 shows how quickly a detector becomes outdated.
  \item WhatsApp was tested with 100 real image pairs and otherwise
        simulated by resizing and JPEG compression; other platforms
        process images differently.
  \item Attribution of current generators (E11) did not transfer from
        the benchmark to images made with the applications themselves.
  \item Each configuration was trained once with a fixed random seed,
        so small differences between methods may not be significant.
  \item Our own test (62 files from 16 generated images) is small, and
        it contains no new real photographs; photorealistic outdoor scenes
        from Gemini were the main weakness.
\end{itemize}

\section{Future Work}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Continual updating: collect images from new generators as they
        are released and retrain, which with frozen CLIP features takes
        minutes once the features are computed.
  \item Attribution that transfers to the applications: training on
        images collected from ChatGPT, Gemini and Grok themselves rather
        than from datasets, and testing on images from other people.
  \item A larger test set collected by ourselves, with images generated
        and photographed by many people and shared through several
        platforms.
  \item Combining evidence: Content Credentials, invisible watermarks
        where detection tools become available, and pixel analysis.
  \item Robustness to deliberate attacks, such as adding noise or
        re-photographing a screen, which this project did not test.
\end{itemize}
